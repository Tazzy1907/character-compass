A Good Man

Bob was a nice man. This wasn't just an opinion held by his wife, Amy, or a handful of close friends; it was a widely acknowledged, verifiable fact, much like the sky being blue or toast being better with butter. He was the sort of man who would not only stop to help you change a flat tyre but would also follow you for a mile afterwards just to make sure the spare was holding up.

This particular Saturday was a testament to his nature. It began, as most Saturdays did, with Bob bringing Amy a cup of tea in bed—a ritual he’d never missed in twelve years of marriage. He’d remembered to buy the expensive oat milk she liked and had warmed the mug beforehand because the house was a little chilly.

"You're a good man, Bob," Amy mumbled, her voice thick with sleep as she took the mug.

"Just a man with a good wife," he replied, kissing her forehead before heading downstairs to start on the day's project: clearing out the garage.

It was a Herculean task. The garage had become a graveyard for forgotten hobbies and good intentions: a dusty treadmill, a half-finished birdhouse, three sets of golf clubs, and boxes of things that neither of them could identify but felt hesitant to throw away.

By lunchtime, they had made a respectable dent. As Bob was hauling a box of old university textbooks to the car for a trip to the charity shop, a young woman jogged past, her face a mask of panic. She was frantically patting her pockets.

"Excuse me," she called out, her voice trembling slightly. "You haven't seen a small, brown leather wallet, have you? I think I might have dropped it."

Bob, ever helpful, immediately stopped what he was doing. "I haven't, I'm sorry. Where do you think you might have lost it?"

Amy watched from the garage doorway. She knew what was coming. The garage would remain half-cleared. Their quiet afternoon would be repurposed.

And so it was. For the next hour, Bob became the lead investigator in the Case of the Missing Wallet. He helped the woman, whose name was Chloe, retrace her entire running route. He peered under bushes, checked behind bins, and even knocked on a few doors to ask if anyone had handed a wallet in. Amy, who had initially stayed behind, eventually joined them, offering a more systematic approach to the search.

Finally, tucked beneath the wheel of a parked car two streets over, Bob spotted it. Chloe could have cried with relief. It contained not just her cards and cash, but a faded photograph of her late grandfather.

"I can't thank you enough!" she gushed, trying to press a twenty-pound note into Bob's hand.

"Absolutely not," Bob said, gently refusing the money. "I'm just glad we could find it. You have a good rest of your day now."

When they got back home, the box of textbooks was still sitting by the car. The sun was beginning to dip, and the motivation to tackle the rest of the garage had vanished.

"Well," Amy said, wiping a smudge of dust from her cheek. "So much for our productive afternoon." There was no annoyance in her voice, only a fond resignation.

Bob looked at her, his expression a little apologetic. "Sorry, Ames. I know we wanted to get it all done today."

She shook her head and walked over to him, wrapping her arms around his waist. "Don't be sorry. That wallet had that picture in it. That was important." She leaned her head against his chest, listening to the steady, reliable beat of his heart. "It’s one of the things I love most about you, you know."

"What is?" he asked, resting his chin on the top of her head.

"You always leave things better than you find them," she said. "Whether it's a stranger's day, a wobbly table at a café, or just my mood in the morning."

Bob smiled, a quiet, genuine smile that reached his eyes. He looked at the half-cleared garage, a job unfinished, and then at his wife, who understood him completely. He felt like the luckiest man in the world.

"The garage can wait," he said softly. "How about we order a pizza and watch that film you wanted to see?"

Amy looked up at him, her eyes sparkling. "I thought you'd never ask."

And as they walked inside, hand in hand, leaving the mess for another day, Amy thought to herself that while Bob was indeed a very nice man, he was, more importantly, a good one. And in a world that often felt chaotic and sharp-edged, living in the warmth of his goodness was the best place to be.

But as they walked inside, hand in hand, Bob’s mind wasn’t on pizza or films. It was on the loose floorboard under the dusty treadmill in the garage. The interaction with Chloe, the frantic jogger, had been a necessary, almost tedious performance. He had to maintain his reputation, to be the helpful neighbour everyone trusted, because trust was the one thing that kept people from looking too closely. He squeezed Amy's hand, drawing comfort from her blissful ignorance. She saw him as her safe harbour, her good man, never suspecting that the real reason for clearing the garage was to finally dispose of what was left of the jogger from last week, the one who hadn't been so lucky as to just lose a wallet.
